◆ The situation

The board approved it, and your name is on it.

Most organizations set the direction and assume the work will specify itself.

So the people building it make dozens of reasonable local decisions every week, and nothing ties those decisions back to what the board approved. Nobody owns the handoffs between teams, and irreversible choices get made by default. People keep their concerns to themselves because raising them feels like standing in front of a moving train.

The activity underway looks like delivery — updates, artifacts, and meetings — right up until you compare what shipped to what was intended. By then the forecasted results aren’t coming, and your credibility goes with them. The next time someone proposes a big bet, the organization is more gun-shy.

The conventional playbook doesn’t solve this. A strong project manager, more town halls, and additional product feature work can all help once the strategy is sound and aligned. Before that, they just paper over the real problem.

When should you call me?

You’re responsible for an initiative you can’t afford to fall short on, and:

  • Every status report is green, but you don’t fully believe it.
  • The business and technology teams define success differently.
  • Key people are going through the motions, or quietly hoping it fails.
  • The launch date is getting closer, and adoption looks less certain.
  • You’re about to commit more money and want an independent read first.

◆ The High-Stakes Initiative Operating System

Every initiative I’ve led has needed the same four steps in the same order, because each step only works if the one before it is done. The full process typically runs 90 to 120 days.

  1. Diagnosis

    Phase 1 · Diagnosis

    Step 1: Get the Real Picture
    Step 2: Surface the Elephants

    I stress-test the business case, talk with intended customers or users, and meet one-on-one with the team and the stakeholders who’ll decide whether it gets adopted.

    What you get:

    • An assessment of what’s working, what’s broken, and what needs to change
    • Explicit success criteria and the assumptions the business case rests on
    • A stakeholder map, and the concerns nobody is raising in the meeting
    • A short list of early possible wins
  2. Alignment

    Phase 2 · Alignment

    Step 3: Align the Plans

    I test the product, technology, and rollout plans against the business case and against each other, and we resolve what doesn’t line up.

    What you get:

    • Product, technology, and rollout strategies aligned to the business case
    • The gaps between them, and a plan to close each one
    • The decisions that need senior attention, surfaced and made
  3. Hand-off

    Phase 3 · Hand-off

    Step 4: Install the Operating System

    We run the operating system together until the initiative has momentum, then I hand it to a named successor.

    What you get:

    • Decision, risk, and assumption registers
    • A weekly operating meeting, a sponsor escalation meeting, and a stakeholder communication rhythm
    • A named owner and a plan for after the engagement ends

◆ Why not handle it another way?

“We have capable people internally.”

Running a function well and holding together a cross-functional initiative take different skills. And everyone you’d assign already has a full-time job keeping the current business running.

“We’ve used consultants and they didn’t get deep enough.”

Most consulting engagements are staffed by people who’ve studied organizations like yours but never run one. I’ve sat in your chair, and I stay in the work until the critical decisions are made and the system runs without me.

“Shouldn’t we hire someone full-time?”

If you have a permanent need and can afford an executive at this level, hiring makes sense. For a finite initiative, most companies can’t justify the headcount, and a search takes time you don’t have.
