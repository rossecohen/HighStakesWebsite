◆ The situation

The people you answer to signed off, and your name is on it.

Most organizations set the direction for a big initiative and assume the work will specify itself.

So the team building it makes dozens of reasonable local decisions every week, and nothing ties those decisions back to the business case. Nobody owns the handoffs between teams, and irreversible choices get made by default. People keep their concerns to themselves because raising them feels like standing in front of a moving train.

The activity underway looks like delivery — updates, artifacts, and meetings — right up until you compare what shipped to what was intended. By then the forecasted results aren’t coming, and your credibility takes the hit. The next time someone proposes a big bet, the organization is more gun-shy.

The conventional playbook doesn’t solve this. A strong project manager, more town halls, and more feature work help only after the strategy is sound and the organization is behind it. Before that, they just paper over the problem. The work that comes first is surfacing and closing the gaps between the vision and expected results.

◆ What I Do

Big initiatives break in the gaps between the vision and the results.

Status reports track milestones, so they stay green while those gaps stay open. Here’s where gaps arise and what I do about each.

  1. ◆

    Vision →to Reality

    What breaks: The plan rests on assumptions nobody has tested against customers or the numbers.

    What I do: I stress-test the business case and talk directly with customers, producers, and your team.

  2. ◆

    Reality →to Strategy

    What breaks: What you’ve learned about customers and the organization never makes it back into the strategy.

    What I do: I rework the strategy and plans around what’s actually true and get the stakeholders behind it.

  3. ◆

    Strategy →to Build

    What breaks: The product and technology plans each make sense alone, but don’t add up to the strategy or support each other.

    What I do: I test the plans against the strategy and each other, and we resolve what doesn’t line up.

  4. ◆

    Build →to Adoption

    What breaks: It ships, and then…silence. The numbers never move.

    What I do: I lead the rollout, enablement, and go-to-market so producers, customers, and staff actually use it.

Close all four gaps and the initiative delivers what the business case promised. Your team keeps the system for the next one.

◆ Why not handle it another way?

“We have capable people internally.”

Running a function well and holding together a cross-functional initiative take different skills. And everyone you’d assign already has a full-time job keeping the current business running.

“We’ve used consultants and they didn’t get deep enough.”

Most consulting engagements are staffed by people who’ve studied organizations like yours but never run one. I’ve sat in your chair, and I stay in the work until the critical decisions are made and the system runs without me.

“Shouldn’t we hire someone full-time?”

If you have a permanent need and can afford an executive at this level, hiring makes sense. For a finite initiative, most companies can’t justify the headcount, and a search takes time you don’t have.
