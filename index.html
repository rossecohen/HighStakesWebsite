◆ The situation

The board approved it. The budget is committed. Your name is on it. Everyone is watching.

Leadership set a vision and assumed that stating the direction was the same as specifying the work. But it isn’t.

So the people building the thing are making dozens of seemingly reasonable local decisions every week, with no mechanism to make sure those decisions ladder up to what the board approved. Handoffs have no owners. Irreversible choices are being made by default. Concerns are being kept private because raising them feels like standing in front of a moving train.

The activity underway looks like delivery — updates, artifacts, and meetings — right up until you compare what shipped to what was intended.

So the big initiative misses the mark. Response is tepid. The forecasted business results don’t occur. Your credibility is burned. And the organization is more gunshy about taking on big challenges in the future.

◆ The Conventional Playbook

The Conventional Playbook can’t cut it. Project managers, one-to-many meetings, and
product focus alone don’t solve these issues.

“Put a strong project manager on it and add structure.”

A project manager gives you documentation and meeting cadences. Those are useful once the strategy has been pressure-tested and the real concerns are on the table. Before that, a project manager makes the activity more organized without making it more correct.

“Communicate more. More town halls, more all-hands.”

Communication flows one direction in a town hall. The problem you have runs the other way: the people building this have concerns they aren’t saying, and the constituencies who feel threatened are expressing it through delay rather than objection. You can’t broadcast your way to candor.

“Get the product right. Nail the features and the experience.”

This one is the most expensive of the three, because everything it sets in motion is downstream work. Feature prioritization and screen design only matter once you’ve answered the questions that decide whether the initiative is worth building at all.

These tools — structure, communication, and product craft — work after an operating system is in place. They make a working system run better, but they can’t substitute for one.

◆ The High-Stakes Initiative Operating System

Every initiative I’ve led in the past twenty years needed the same four things, in the same order. They build on each other and compound; each step only works if the one before it is done.

  1. 1

    Step 1.

    Get the Real Picture.

    Understand the strategy and challenge it before another dollar is spent. Read the business case the way a skeptical investor would, stress-test the economics, and talk directly to the intended customers or users instead of relying on internal conviction about what they want.

  2. 2

    Step 2.

    Surface the Elephants.

    Get real buy-in from the team and the stakeholders. Structured, individual conversations with the people building this and the people who’ll decide whether it gets adopted. Map who’s on board, who’s waiting, and who reads this as a threat to their standing, and get those concerns on the table and resolved before the resistance goes underground.

  3. 3

    Step 3.

    Propagate the Strategy.

    Align the product, technology, and rollout strategies so they support the executive vision and each other. Test the product plan against what leadership approved and what customers actually need. Test the tech and rollout plans against the product plan, and resolve what doesn’t line up.

  4. 4

    Step 4.

    Build and Run the OS.

    Put in place the system that keeps this moving after the initial push: owners for every seam, not just the workstreams. Explicit decision rights. A mechanism for surfacing and resolving issues and concerns quickly, one people actually trust and use. A cadence for how information flows and a process for how decisions get made. Then we run it together until the initiative has momentum, and I hand ownership over to a named successor.

◆ Frequently Asked Questions before Booking

“We have capable people internally.”

Yes, you do; capable at the jobs they were hired to do. Running a function well and holding together a cross-functional initiative are different skill sets, and organizations conflate them regularly. Every person you’d assign already has a full-time job keeping the current business running.

“How fast can you get up to speed?”

The diagnosis is a key part of the engagement, not preparation for it. I’ve spent twenty years in insurance, lending, and financial services, so I’m not learning your industry. I’m learning about your organization.

“We’ve used consultants and they didn’t get deep enough.”

Most consulting engagements are staffed by people who’ve studied organizations like yours but never run one. I’ve sat in your chair and been responsible for delivering results through multiple high-stakes initiatives at once. I don’t just hand you a deck. I get the right people aligned, make sure the critical decisions get made, and build the system so it runs after I’m gone.

“Shouldn’t we hire someone full-time?”

If you can afford an executive at this level permanently and have a permanent need, that’s a reasonable approach. Many companies can’t justify the hire for a finite initiative, and a search takes time you don’t have. This approach gives you seniority and focus for the duration you need immediately, without the permanent headcount.
